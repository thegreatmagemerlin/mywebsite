Open Differential

Designed in SolidWorks and printed in PLA, the differential covers bevel gear design, assembly constraints, and iterative prototyping.

CAD Mechanical 3D Printing

Overview

This is a fully 3D-printed open differential, designed from scratch in SolidWorks and built as a functional mechanical prototype. It was originally intended as a drivetrain component for an RC car, a stepping stone project before eventually tackling a drone build.

The project covers mechanical CAD assembly, bevel gear design, tolerance analysis, and iterative prototyping with FDM printing. The core mechanism works, though the prototype isn't vehicle-ready yet the housing needs refinement and the motor needs upgrading before it can go into an actual car.

The problem

An open differential allows two output shafts to rotate at different speeds while receiving torque from a single input. This matters because when a car corners, the outer wheel travels a larger arc than the inner wheel. If both wheels are forced to spin at the same speed, you get tyre scrubbing and drivetrain stress. A differential lets each wheel rotate at whatever speed it needs to.

The goal was to design and build a working one from scratch. Not to use an off-the-shelf unit, but to actually understand the mechanism by building it.

The engineering challenge broke down into three parts: getting the gear assembly designed and mated correctly in SolidWorks, translating that into physically printable parts with real-world tolerances, and figuring out how to actually assemble something with components that depend on each other.

How it works

An open differential has four main components: a ring gear driven by the pinion (input from the motor), a carrier that rotates with the ring gear, two spider gears mounted inside the carrier on a cross-shaft, and two axle gears connected to each output shaft.

In a straight line, the spider gears don't rotate relative to the carrier. They lock the two axle gears together and torque splits evenly. In a corner, resistance builds on one wheel, which causes the spider gears to rotate on their own axis, allowing the two axle gears to turn at different speeds while the carrier keeps receiving input torque.

The gear ratio between the pinion and ring gear sets the torque multiplication and speed reduction from motor to axle. For this prototype, the ratio was chosen to balance printability (larger teeth are more forgiving to manufacture) with reasonable speed reduction for the motor being used.

Engineering challenges

SolidWorks Assembly Constraints
The bevel gears from the SolidWorks Design Toolbox are generated as assemblies, not parts. That became a problem once I needed to add custom geometry (likeshafts, spider gear mounts) because those modifications required editable part files. Converting an assembly to a part in SolidWorks strips the parametric features and breaks the existing mates, since reference geometry doesn't carry over cleanly.

The workaround: build the complete assembly with all mates intact first, then convert each gear to a part, add the required custom geometry to each one, and re-import and re-mate everything from scratch in the main assembly. It took a while, but keeping clean file names and a consistent process made it manageable.

FDM Printing and Anisotropy
The first axle shafts broke quickly. They'd been printed vertically along the shaft axis, which put the gear interface perpendicular to the layer lines, which was the worst possible orientation for torsional load on an FDM part. They snapped cleanly at the joint under minimal force.

The fix was redesigning the connection as a keyed slot: a profiled cut in the gear bore that the shaft seats into mechanically. This moved load transfer from layer adhesion to geometry, which is much more reliable. Re-orienting the print to improve layer adhesion in the load direction helped further. A hand-twist test confirmed the redesigned joint was strong enough to be usable.

Bearing Fit and Tolerances
The ball bearings (I bought this time as printed bearings aren't appropriate for precision rotating parts) required accurate shaft diameters to get a proper fit. Without callipers, I had to print a series of test shafts at incremental sizes to find what worked empirically. The 0.2mm nozzle helped here as smaller layer lines gave better dimensional accuracy on the shaft and gear tooth geometry.

Assembly Sequence Problem
During assembly, I ran into a sequencing issue: the spider gears couldn't be inserted once the axle gears were in place, and the axle gears couldn't go in if the spider gears were already mounted. Everything was geometrically locked with no valid assembly order.

The fix was to split the spider gear mounts into separate pieces that could be assembled in stages and then connected with tab inserts. This is a fairly standard design-for-assembly consideration, and one I should have thought through at the CAD stage.

Housing Tolerances
The housing took the most iterations. The first three prints either clamped the internals too tight to move, or had misaligned holes that wouldn't accept fasteners. Without callipers, each iteration meant printing the full housing and testing by assembly, then adjusting geometry based on what failed. The current version fits and assembles correctly, but is slightly loose on the pinion bore, which is fine on the bench but probably not reliable enough under vibration or real load.

Results

The prototype demonstrates correct differential action: the ring and pinion mesh properly, the spider gears allow independent rotation between the two output axles, and the assembly can be taken apart and put back together repeatedly. It works as intended under light, static conditions.

It's not vehicle-ready. The pinion bore in the housing is too loose to maintain reliable mesh under dynamic load, and the motor (a basic Arduino kit DC motor on a 1S LiPo) is undersized for driving an actual chassis at useful speeds.

What the project did accomplish was going through the full design-to-manufacture cycle for a complex mechanical assembly, and running into most of the manufacturing constraints that CAD doesn't show you.

Future improvements

Improvements could include:

  • Housing redesign: Tighter bore tolerances on the pinion shaft, ideally with a proper bearing seat rather than a printed hole. Using callipers throughout would remove a lot of the guesswork.
  • Motor upgrade: Something with enough torque and speed range for the drivetrain gear ratio, rather than a generic kit motor.
  • Steering geometry: If this becomes a full RC car, front axle steering (Ackermann geometry) is the next mechanical problem to solve.
  • Callipers: Just buy them.
